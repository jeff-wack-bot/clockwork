Sample Factory

    
Blocks and wires, like a rack. Blocks have pins: inputs on the left, outputs on the right (on an analog module's panel, its jacks). Drag from a pin to a pin to connect them with a wire, the way you patch a cable. An output can feed any number of inputs; an input takes one wire, and a new one replaces it. Drag a wire's end off an input to move it, or drop it on empty board to remove it.

    
Three kinds of signal, one canvas. Float pins carry numbers and their wires are drawn as copper traces, coloured by value. Fixed-point pins carry words of bits and their wires are barcodes: one stripe per bit, white for 1 and black for 0, live. Analog jacks carry voltages and their wires are patch cables that hang between them. A wire only joins pins of the same kind; converters cross over: ADC and DAC (analog ↔ fixed), Quantizer and Dequantizer (float ↔ fixed), Voltmeter and AWG (analog ↔ float), and the pitch converters.

    
How wires look is yours. Select a wire: give it a colour; a trace its width, a cable its droop. Double-click a trace to put a via there (the trace changes layer); until you place vias, they are drawn wherever traces cross. To shape a trace or a barcode, drag it sideways, or draw it corner by corner with Draw wire (W). Moving a block drags its wires along.

    
Fixed point. The gates (AND, OR, XOR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XNOR) work on every bit of a bus at once; a bus is as wide as what drives it. Split takes a bus apart into fields and Join puts fields together. The D flip-flop is the register. Where the binary point is, and whether a word is signed, is up to the converters: the bus is only bits.

    
Analog modules sit on the board as their panels, knobs and all: VCOs, a ladder filter, VCAs, envelopes, LFOs, noise, a mixer, an analog sequencer. Turn a knob by dragging it (double-click resets it). They run on the same clock as everything else: a tick is always 1/48 000 s of simulated time, so slowing the clock puts them in slow motion too. Each is sampled at the clock edge (one tick of latency), so feedback through them is always allowed. Voltages: 1 V/oct with 0 V = C4, audio ±5 V, gates 0–10 V.

    
Sockets. In and Out blocks are the design's audio inputs and outputs. In the browser the first Out is the left speaker and the second the right (one Out plays on both); Ins hear the microphone. With the PipeWire bridge (run python3 tools/pwbridge.py as yourself), the design is a node in PipeWire with a port per socket, to patch in Helvum, and the other programs' nodes can be shown and patched on the board.

    
Flip-flops are the registers. The Delay IC is a flip-flop: its output only changes at the clock edge. Every feedback loop needs one; a loop without one is an error. The right-hand panel shows the critical path: the longest chain of logic between flip-flops.

    
Constants. A Constant block is a number, with a knob if you like; wire it to any number of inputs. Inside a block, a Constant (or an input's default, set in the block's inspector) is a default: a wire from outside overrides it.

    
Style lab. The Style menu changes how operations, branch points, direction and registers are drawn. Compare them side by side.

    
Zoom is navigation. Every composite IC shows its own board inside. Scroll in until its traces are big and you are working inside it. The definition is shared, but each instance keeps its own knob values. Double-click an IC to fly into it, Z or Esc to back out, H to see everything.

    
One clock. The slider runs the design from 0.5 ticks/s up to real time (48 000 ticks/s, one per audio sample). With audio on, every Out holds its value between ticks, so slow clocks are clicks and real time is sound.

    
Selecting and editing. With Select (V), drag on empty board to box-select; click a block or a trace to select it; Shift-click adds or removes. Drag a selected block to move the selection, or nudge it with the arrow keys. Copy, cut, paste and duplicate take blocks and the wires between them, keeping knobs intact. Every change can be undone.
